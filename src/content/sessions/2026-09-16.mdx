## The order of the block, which is the part worth keeping

The session opened with *"first internal."* It was pushed back on, and the reason is on the record
three days earlier — [the phase 2 decision](/sessions/2026-09-13) named exactly this:

> **Phase 1's coding was a first-class track on paper too, and internals kept winning the block.**

[Merge Two Sorted Lists](/coding/24) had already slipped from Tuesday. With 60 minutes available,
internals-first meant it very likely slipped again. The pushback distinguished **a real reason** —
mid-thought on something, a work need — **from a preference**, and offered to proceed either way.

**The user switched to coding first without argument, and the problem got done.** Had the order
held, week 4 would be two problems behind rather than one.

What makes this worth [its own item](/open#internals-first-on-day-three-of-phase-2) rather than a
line in the note: **the failure mode was predicted correctly at the phase boundary and appeared on
day three.** It will appear again, because the pull that produced it has not changed. And the thing
that worked was **naming it at the moment it happened** — not the plan text that anticipated it in
writing, which is the same lesson as [start the clock before reading](/open#timed-and-narrated-committed-day-one-never-once-done):
an intention does not interrupt anything, a step you cannot skip does.

## One bug, not several

The diagnosis in the room was *"couple of bugs."* There was one, and it produced all the symptoms.

```swift
// resultNode is doing two jobs: the head to return, AND the append point
resultNode?.next = node   // ← the next iteration overwrites this same .next
```

**Every append clobbers the previous one**, leaving a two-node list. Two pointers are needed: one
that never moves, and one that walks forward as you append.

That is also where the second correction lives. The read offered was *"it was lengthy, that's why I
introduced a bug."* **Backwards** — the length came *from* the missing idea:

| Missing | What it forces |
|---|---|
| The dummy node | A first-append check in **every branch** |
| `<=` rather than `<` | A **third branch** for the equal case |

Each one duplicates the append block. **The dummy did not shorten a correct solution; it removed the
condition that was forcing the duplication.** Which generalises into something you can say out loud
in an interview: *"I'm repeating this check in every branch — there's probably a way to make it
unnecessary."* That sentence is often what gets you to the dummy node unaided.

## The two SwiftUI answers that were one answer

Three of four substantially correct, against two of five on Tuesday. The interesting part is not the
score but **the direction the two partial answers missed in**, which was the same direction twice.

| | What was held | What was missing |
|---|---|---|
| **Identity** | Drives diffing — a performance concern | **It decides whether `@State` survives** |
| **`AnyView`** | Erases the type, costs dynamic dispatch | **It erases what identity is read from** |

Both were held as *performance* facts about a mechanism whose real consequence is **lifetime**. When
identity changes, SwiftUI does not update the view — it decides this is a different view, tears the
old one down and discards its state. Same pixels on screen, state silently reset.

And the two answers close each other's loop: **`AnyView`'s real cost is paid in the same currency**,
because it erases the static type that identity is read from. Neither half was connected to the
other when asked cold, which is the finding worth carrying rather than either individual miss. It also sharpens
[the `AnyView?` struct parked on 12 Sep](/open#the-anyview-struct-deferred), whose third option —
remove the erasure — was already listed as the only one that solves the problem rather than placing
it. This is the reason why.
