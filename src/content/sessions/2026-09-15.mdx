## The result worth keeping, and it is not the code

The solution is eight lines and standard. What makes Monday-into-Tuesday worth recording is the
sequence:

| | |
|---|---|
| **Mon 14 Sep** | Eighteen minutes stuck. Structure supplied. No timer, nothing narrated |
| **Tue 15 Sep** | **Written cold from an empty file.** Four lines, right order, `return prev` |

**This is a better test of trace-then-rewrite than anything it has been used on before.**
[The 424 argument](/open#the-424-stale-maxfreq-safety-argument) — the method's previous showcase —
was re-derived on material that had already been solved once. This topic had *never been touched*.
Stuck, supplied, then produced cold after a day's gap is a genuinely different claim, and it is the
first evidence that the method works on new ground rather than on recovery.

## Why the saved pointer is the problem, not a detail

The orienting difficulty, and the reason array intuition fails here:

```swift
// standing on node 2
node2.next = node1    // the arrow flips...
                      // ...and the only reference to node 3 is gone
```

**The list is cut.** So `let next = current?.next` is not a tidy-up or a micro-optimisation — it is
what makes the operation possible at all. Saying that out loud is the difference between
remembering four lines and being able to re-derive them, which is exactly what Tuesday tested.

## The fake was honest, and that is the second time in a week

Named before the code, as it has been for five of the last six problems: *collect the values into an
array, then build a new list.*

**It returns the correct list.** Every correctness test passes. What it gets wrong is the
constraint — `O(n)` extra space and *n* fresh nodes, against relinking the existing ones at `O(1)`.

| Date | The fake | Differs in |
|---|---|---|
| 9 Sep | [Append and linear-scan behind a heap's API](/internals#17-heaps) | **Cost only** |
| 15 Sep | Collect into an array, rebuild the list | **Cost only** |

> **The plausible wrong answer is usually right and expensive, not wrong.**

That inverts every earlier entry on [the testing item](/open#distinguishing-input), where the work
was finding an input that produces a *different answer*. And it means the technique keeps landing in
the one case it least covers — [the extension named on 9 Sep](/open#heap-tests-that-a-linear-scan-would-also-pass),
*stop testing behaviour and count work*, has now been the right move on two consecutive problems and
has still never been run.

## The reversed pair, and the pattern it completes

`setNeedsLayout` and `layoutIfNeeded` came out backwards — the flag described as forcing, the force
as deferring. It was corrected in-session, which is why it is a correction rather than a gap, and
why it cannot close on that correction.

**A gap announces itself. A reversal feels like knowledge**: fluent, confident, and it produces code
that looks right and fails in exactly one way — the constraint animation that snaps instead of
animating, because layout ran at the end of the run loop rather than inside the block.

Third instance of one shape in eight days:

| Date | Held backwards |
|---|---|
| 8 Sep | [Two-pointer directions](/open#two-pointer-directions-inverted) — sum too large moved *left* rightward |
| 10 Sep | [Max-heap for the k *most* frequent](/open#top-k-min-heap-or-max-heap) |
| 15 Sep | `setNeeds…` against `…IfNeeded` |

The trigger is the same in all three and is now worth naming: **the English pulls the wrong way.**
"Most frequent" suggests a max-heap. "Set needs layout" sounds like an instruction to do it. In each
case the structure was retrieved correctly and the direction was filled in by the reading the words
suggested — which is the signature of pattern-matching rather than deriving.

## Week 4, one behind, and why that is being watched rather than fixed

Merge Two Sorted Lists did not run. It moves to Wednesday, which leaves the week one short on a
five-problem plan.

**Five a week is roughly double the rate of the first three weeks**, so a shortfall was predictable
rather than surprising. The decision to drop it rather than compress two days into one evening is
the right one, and it is the same reasoning that retired
[the two-problem test](/open#two-problems-at-ninety-minutes) on 1 Sep: throughput was never the
bottleneck.

**If week 4 lands three or four, that is the real rate** — and the honest response is to resize
[phase 2's curriculum](/plan) rather than squeeze the sessions, because squeezing is what produced
the one-topic board in the first place.
